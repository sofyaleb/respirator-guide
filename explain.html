How a protection factor works

The number on a mask's rating is easy to misread. Six short steps explain what it means, why fit matters more than the filter, and how time adds up. Swipe, use the arrows or press → to move on.

1 · The idea

It's a ratio: outside divided by inside

A protection factor compares how much is in the air around you with how much reaches you behind the mask.

Protection factor = particles outside ÷ particles inside

So a mask with a protection factor of 5 lets in about one fifth of what's in the air. Out of every 100 particles you would have breathed, about 20 still get in.

2 · Reading the numbers

20× isn't "twice as safe" as 10×

Going from no mask to 10× cuts what you breathe from 100 particles to 10. Going from 10× to 20× cuts it from 10 to 5.

Both steps halve or better what gets in, but the first step does most of the work. That's why any well-fitted respirator is a big improvement over a loose mask, and why the jump to powered respirators matters mainly for heavy, all-day exposure.

3 · Filter vs leaks

Most of what gets in comes round the edges

Air reaches you two ways: through the filter, or through gaps where the mask meets your face. Breathing pulls air along whichever path is easiest.

An FFP2 filter lets through at most 6% and an FFP3 at most 1%, but both are rated at roughly 10× to 20× overall, because a few percent always leaks at the nose, cheeks and chin. Past a certain point a better filter barely helps. A better seal does.

4 · Fit factor

What a fit factor is

A fit factor measures how well one mask seals on one person's face. It compares the particles in the room with the particles that end up inside the mask.

Fit factor = particles in the room ÷ particles inside the mask

A fit factor of 200 means that for every 200 particles in the room, only 1 made it inside, whether through the filter or round the edges.

It differs from a protection factor in two ways. It is measured during a short test rather than in everyday wear. And it belongs to you and that mask: the same mask can score 300 on one face and 20 on another.

5 · Passing a fit test

Passing a fit test, and the official rating

In a fit test you wear the mask for a few minutes while breathing normally, breathing deeply, talking, turning your head and bending over. The counter works out a fit factor for each movement and combines them into one overall score, which the weakest movements pull down the most.

To pass, a half mask or disposable respirator usually needs a fit factor of at least 100. Yet its official assigned protection factor is only 10. That gap is deliberate: it allows for masks shifting, beards, talking and imperfect wear in real life.

6 · Time adds up

A mask slows the clock

The longer you breathe the same air, the more you take in. A protection factor works like slowing that clock down: in a 5× mask, every 5 minutes is equal to 1 minute with no mask.

Time spent ÷ protection factor = the same as this long with no mask

An 8-hour flight in a disposable respirator (10×) adds up to about 48 minutes unmasked. In a face mask (about 3×) the same flight adds up to about 2 hours 40 minutes.

Two things make the clock run faster: more particles in the air, as in crowded, stuffy rooms or smoke, and breathing harder, as when exercising or talking loudly. That is where a better-fitting, higher-protection mask makes the biggest difference.
