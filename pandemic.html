Good respirators could stop even a worst-case pandemic

If the people who have to keep working all wore well-fitting respirators, and everyone else stayed home for a while, even a very contagious, very deadly airborne virus would stop spreading, buying time for vaccines.

This page explains the argument in “Universal respirator usage stops worst-case pandemics” by Constantin Arnscheidt and Damon Binder (Defenses in Depth, October 2026), in plain language. Unless marked as this guide's official ratings, the numbers are theirs; the interactive tools are simplified from their model.

1 · Why plan for the worst case

COVID-19 was bad. It was not the worst case.

COVID-19 killed tens of millions of people, but the original virus killed roughly 1 in 200 of the people it infected. Other diseases have been far deadlier. SARS in 2003 killed about 1 in 10 of those infected, but it spread less easily and was contained after fewer than 10,000 cases. Smallpox was both contagious and deadly: early epidemics may have killed about a third of the population in some places.

The authors worry that advances in biotechnology and AI could make it easier to engineer a virus that is both. Keeping that hard to do matters most, but it is worth having a plan if it happens anyway.

Vaccines take months to develop and make at scale, and treatments usually take longer. Something has to hold the line in between. The authors argue that respirators can do it, because they work against any airborne virus and can be made and stored before we know what we are facing.

2 · The goal

Push R below 1 and the outbreak dies out

R is how many people, on average, each infected person goes on to infect. Above 1, cases multiply each generation. Below 1, each generation is smaller than the last and the outbreak fizzles out. R₀ is the value when nobody is immune and nobody is protecting themselves: roughly 1.5 to 6.5 for the original COVID-19 virus, 3.5 to 6 for smallpox and 12 to 18 for measles.

A mask cuts R in two ways. It stops some of what you breathe in. And if the infected person wears one too, it stops some of what they breathe out. When both wear one, the two effects multiply:

Everyone wearing a mask with protection factor PF cuts R by PF × PF

That is why universal wearing is so powerful: a group protection factor of 18, worn by everyone, cuts transmission about 324 times. Try it:

3 · Who needs protecting

Most people stay home. Essential workers mask up.

An airborne virus has to get from one person to another through shared air. If every household kept to itself, it would stop. But someone has to keep the power on, move food and medicine, run hospitals and make the vaccines. In the US that is somewhere between 10 and 30 million people.

So the authors' “minimum viable” plan is:

  1. As soon as a dangerous new virus is spotted, people who don't need to go to work stay home.
  2. Essential workers keep working, wearing good respirators all the time they share air, so that R among them stays below 1.
  3. This holds until vaccines, treatments or better tests arrive.

Staying home is costly, so it only makes sense for a truly severe virus. The harder part is keeping essential workers safe enough, and feeling safe enough, to keep showing up.

4 · How it spreads

The worst case is probably airborne

A virus that spreads fast enough to be a worst case will most likely spread through the air. It is much harder to avoid breathing shared air than to avoid contaminated food, touching people or mosquito bites.

People breathe out particles of all sizes. Big ones fall to the ground within a few metres; small ones float and drift across a room for much longer. The old dividing line between “droplets” and “airborne” at 5 micrometres is arbitrary: respiratory viruses travel both ways. COVID-19, flu, colds and measles all spread substantially through the air, and smallpox probably did too.

So the priority is to block airborne spread. Once that is under control, the other routes, such as surfaces and close contact, are easier to deal with. Respirators help there too: they stop hands touching the mouth and nose, and hand washing covers the moments the mask is off.

5 · The tool

How respirators get there

A protection factor is how many times less you breathe in: a mask with a protection factor of 20 lets in about 1 in 20 of the particles in the air. Two things limit it:

  • Leaks round the edges. If 1 in 20 breaths of air slips past the seal, the mask can never do better than 20×, however good the filter. A fit test and some practice putting it on help a lot.
  • Time off. Taking it off counts the same way. A mask worn 95% of the time in shared air can never do better than 20×, because the other 5% gets in unfiltered.

Official ratings, the ones the rest of this guide uses, are deliberately cautious: they are what regulators are confident nearly every worker will get. Studies that measure groups of people wearing respirators through a working day usually find more. The number that matters for stopping spread is the group's average, worked out so that a few badly fitting masks pull it down a lot.

Breathe-out valves undo half the benefit. A valve lets the wearer's breath out unfiltered, so it gives up the “infected person wears one too” half of PF × PF. Pick a mask without a valve, or one with a filter over the valve.

6 · The numbers

How much protection is enough?

The authors built a deliberately pessimistic model of an infectious worker at their most contagious, sharing an office for 50 hours a week, and asked how high the group protection factor has to be for R to stay below 1. They assumed people who are visibly ill stay home. Because everyone is wearing a mask, the protection factor needed is the square root of how contagious the virus could be in that setting.

How infectious the virus isUpper bound on spread at workGroup protection factor needed
As infectious as the worst known viruses, like measlesup to ~320 people per caseabout 18×

1,000 times more: the authors' strongest upper bound for any virus~4,000about 64×

Even the most extreme case needs about 64×, which a well-fitted reusable respirator can reach. And a protection factor well below 18 would have been enough to stop the original COVID-19 virus, if people had worn good respirators consistently. During COVID, most people wore loose surgical masks, some of the time. Wearing consistently is the hard part, though the authors expect people to be far more motivated against a virus that kills a large share of the people it infects.

7 · What about cleaner air?

Air cleaning helps, but can't do it alone

Cleaning indoor air also cuts how much virus people breathe in. The options are better ventilation, air filters (including cheap DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes), far-UVC lamps that kill viruses in the air, and low levels of glycol vapour. Realistic ventilation and filtering upgrades cut what you breathe in by up to about 5×. Far-UVC might reach 20–40× within safe light levels, and glycol vapours look modest. Cutting exposure 900× with ventilation alone would mean replacing all the air in a room 900 times an hour, far beyond even an operating theatre.

They do stack with masks, though: a 4× cut from cleaner air brings the protection factor needed from 30× down to about 15×. Combine them and see which viruses you could stop:

8 · Cost

A stockpile would be cheap

Disposable respirators cost about $1 each and last a few days of wear. A reusable elastomeric half mask costs about $30 and lasts years. If an emergency lasts more than about three months, the reusable mask is cheaper, and it protects better. That makes elastomeric masks with P100 filters the authors' pick for a stockpile.

For the US, 20 million elastomeric masks would cost about $600 million. Add as much again for storage and handing them out, spread it over a 20-year shelf life, and it comes to roughly $60 million a year: under 0.001% of federal spending. 9 · For you

What it means if you're choosing a mask

A reusable elastomeric mask is the sweet spot

With P100 or P3 filters it protects more than a disposable respirator, and over months it costs less. It is what the authors would stockpile.

Fit matters more than the filter

Most of what gets in comes round the edges. Get fit tested if you can, use head straps rather than ear loops, and pinch the nose clip.

Wear it all the time you share air

Ten minutes off in a crowded room can undo hours of protection. Consistency is what drives R below 1.

Skip the valve if you might be ill

A mask without a breathe-out valve protects the people around you too, which is half of the PF × PF effect.

10 · Caveats

What the argument assumes

  • The model is deliberately pessimistic and isn't fitted to any one disease. It assumes air in a room mixes evenly and that the virus doesn't decay in the air, both of which overstate spread.
  • It assumes visibly ill workers stay home. How much time people spend talking, coughing and so on is the authors' own estimate.
  • The measured protection factors come from small studies, mostly of trained workers. Full-day results for untrained people are limited or unpublished.
  • Reaching 18× or more across millions of people depends on them wearing masks consistently, which COVID showed is hard.
  • The cost figures are rough and leave out training, fit testing and logistics. The cost of people staying home is real but isn't worked out here.
  • Vaccines, treatments and tests can't be made in advance for an unknown virus, and the model doesn't cover diseases that spread mainly in other ways.
