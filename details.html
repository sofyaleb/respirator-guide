Respirator protection compared

A well-fitted FFP2 or N95 cuts the particles you breathe in by about —. A surgical mask manages roughly —, because air leaks round its edges. How well a mask seals to your face matters as much as the label.

Surgical mask — Estimate from fit studies. Designed to stop splashes and the wearer's droplets, not to seal.
FFP2 · N95 · KN95 — The everyday respirator tier. Different countries' versions are broadly equivalent.
FFP3 — The highest disposable class in Europe, used for high-risk healthcare and dusty work.
Powered respirator — A fan pushes filtered air into a tight half mask. Workplace equipment, not everyday wear.

Reading the table and labels

New to protection factors and fit tests? How protection works explains them step by step.

Filter efficiency
The share of test particles the filter material stops in the lab. It ignores air that leaks round the edges.
Total inward leakage
Everything that gets past the mask on a real person: through the filter, round the face seal and through any valve. European and several Asian standards test this; the US rating tests the filter only and relies on fit testing at work.
N, R and P
In US ratings the letter is about oil. N is not resistant to oily particles, R is somewhat resistant and P is strongly resistant. For everyday air, smoke and viruses, N is fine.
NR and R markings
On EU masks, NR means single shift only and R means it can be reused. A D marking means it passed an extra clogging test with dolomite dust.

Getting a good fit

What breaks a seal

Facial hair under the edge, the wrong size or shape for your face, loose or crossed straps, and a nose clip that has not been pinched to shape.

An FFP3 that gaps can protect less than an FFP2 that seals.

Head straps, not ear loops

Ear loops pull the edges away from your cheeks. In a review of twenty-nine fit-testing studies, only about four in a hundred ear-loop fit tests passed, against about eighty in a hundred for folded respirators with head straps such as the 3M Aura.

In the US, ear-loop designs are not approved as N95s. In the UK, the HSE has warned against them and the NHS does not use them.

A quick fit check

Cup your hands over the mask and breathe out sharply. If you feel air at the edges or your eyes, adjust the nose clip and straps and try again.

Breathe in hard: a well-sealed mask pulls slightly towards your face.

Ear loops and the rules

An FFP2 with ear loops uses the same filter as one with head straps, but it rarely seals. Here is where the rules stand, from Max Görlitz's post on ear-loop FFP2s.

United States

NIOSH does not approve ear-loop designs as N95s. A certified N95 has head-and-neck straps, unless it was approved with a fastener that joins the loops behind the head.

United Kingdom

The government restricted EN 149 for ear-loop designs, the HSE issued a warning in 2022 and the NHS has not used them since 2020. They can still be sold through recognition of the EU CE mark.

European Union

Ear-loop FFP2s can still carry a CE mark. The post argues the EU should restrict EN 149 as the UK did and phase ear-loop designs out.

Extender clips

Many ear-loop FFP2s come with a clip that joins the loops behind your head. Readers point out they are rarely used. The post's author notes that NIOSH could in principle certify an ear-loop N95 together with such a clip, but he has not found one that has been.

Comfort and valves

From the comments: flat plastic head straps can pull on long hair, and braided elastic straps are often comfier. One reader suggests the 3M Aura 9210+ (no valve) or 9211+ (valve).

A valve makes breathing out easier, but your own breath leaves unfiltered, so it protects others less. Skip the valve when you are ill.

What a good fit looks like

For folded head-strap respirators such as the 3M Aura, the post reports that over ninety-five in a hundred people reach a protection factor above ten, and over eighty in a hundred above thirty. That figure comes from unpublished testing data the author has seen.
